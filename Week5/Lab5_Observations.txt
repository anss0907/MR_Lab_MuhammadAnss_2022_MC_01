Observations on Discrepancies Between Expected and Simulated Motion
====================================================================
Lab 5 - MCT-454L Mobile Robotics
Muhammad Anss | April 18, 2026
====================================================================

During the simulation, I noticed several differences between what I
expected the robot to do and what actually happened in Gazebo. Here
are my key observations:


1. Odometry Drift
-----------------
When I tried to teleop the robot back to (0, 0, 0), the odometry
readings never perfectly returned to zero. Even though I tried to
retrace my path carefully, the final position was slightly off.
This happens because wheel odometry accumulates small errors over
time — every tiny wheel slip or rounding error adds up. This is a
well-known limitation of differential-drive robots and is exactly
why SLAM exists — it corrects for this kind of drift using sensor
data.


2. The Robot Doesn't Stop Instantly
-----------------------------------
When I ran my cmd_vel publisher that alternates between 0.2 m/s and
0.0 m/s every 2 seconds, I expected the robot to immediately jump
between those two speeds. But in Gazebo, the robot takes a moment to
speed up and a moment to slow down. This is because the physics
engine simulates real-world inertia — the robot has mass, and mass
resists changes in velocity. So even though I'm commanding an
instant speed change, the actual motion is gradual. In a real robot,
this would be even more noticeable.


3. Small Velocity When Stationary
---------------------------------
Even when the robot was completely still (no teleop commands being
sent), the odom subscriber showed tiny non-zero velocities like
0.0004 m/s or angular velocities of 0.0089 rad/s. At first I
thought something was wrong, but this is just numerical noise from
the physics simulation. Gazebo uses iterative solvers for physics,
and they produce these tiny residual values. In practice, you'd
typically threshold these out (ignore anything below 0.001 or so).


4. LiDAR Scan Gaps
-------------------
While building the map with Cartographer, I noticed that corners
and narrow spaces sometimes had small gaps or artifacts in the map.
This is because the LiDAR has a fixed angular resolution and range,
so areas that were only scanned briefly or from far away didn't get
enough data points for a clean map. Driving slowly and making
multiple passes through these areas improved the map quality.


5. Orientation Values
---------------------
The orientation in the odom messages is given as a quaternion
(x, y, z, w), which isn't super intuitive to read. For example,
turning 90 degrees doesn't just show up as "90" — instead you see
changes in the z and w components of the quaternion. Converting to
Euler angles would make it more human-readable, but the raw
quaternion avoids issues like gimbal lock. I noticed the z component
steadily changed as I rotated the robot, which confirmed that the
odometry was tracking rotation correctly.


6. cmd_vel vs Actual Motion Mismatch
-------------------------------------
When publishing 0.2 m/s to /cmd_vel, the actual velocity reported
by /odom was usually something like 0.198 or 0.201 — close but not
exactly 0.2. This small discrepancy is because /cmd_vel is a
velocity *command* (what we want), while /odom reports the
*estimated* actual velocity after physics simulation. There are
always small differences due to wheel traction, surface friction,
and other factors simulated by Gazebo.


Summary
-------
Overall, these discrepancies are completely normal and expected in
both simulation and real robotics. They highlight why closed-loop
control, sensor fusion, and SLAM are essential — you can't just
blindly trust odometry or assume commands execute perfectly. The
simulation actually did a good job of mimicking the kinds of
imperfections you'd see on a real TurtleBot3.
